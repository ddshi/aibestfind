I Wasn't Sure What to Expect From a "Notebook" AI

When Google dropped NotebookLM, my first thought was: "Isn't this just Google Docs with a chatbot sidebar?" I wasn't entirely wrong — but I also wasn't right about what that actually means in practice.

Here's the thing. I read a lot. Research papers, blog posts, technical documentation, meeting notes, whitepapers. And I have a problem that I suspect a lot of knowledge workers share: I collect more information than I actually process. My bookmarks folder is a graveyard of "I'll read this later" links that never got read.

NotebookLM was designed specifically for this problem. It's not a general-purpose AI assistant. It's an AI research tool that only works with sources you give it. You upload documents, paste links, add notes — and then the AI helps you understand, summarize, and connect everything in that personal knowledge base.

⚡ Quick Verdict

NotebookLM is the best tool I've used for processing a pile of source material into structured understanding. It's genuinely great at summarizing, cross-referencing, and question-answering across your documents. But it's not a general-purpose note-taking app (it won't replace Notion), and it's useless without good source material. Think of it as a research accelerator, not a note-taking replacement.

The Moments Where NotebookLM Genuinely Impressed Me

1. Source-Grounded Answers (No Hallucinations — Mostly)

This is NotebookLM's killer feature. Every answer it gives comes with inline citations that link back to the exact passage in your source document. Click the citation number, and it jumps to the specific paragraph in the PDF or web page.

I asked it: "What are the key differences between these three AI image generators based on what I've uploaded?" It gave me a comparison table with citations pointing to six different documents. I verified each citation. All correct. This level of source accountability is something ChatGPT and Claude can't do natively — they might cite sources if you ask, but they can hallucinate the citation content.

That said, it's not perfect. When sources contradicted each other, NotebookLM sometimes smoothed over the contradiction instead of flagging it. I had to prompt it specifically: "Are there any contradictions between these two papers?" to get it to acknowledge conflicts.

2. The "Audio Overview" Feature Is Weirdly Useful

Okay, this one surprised me. NotebookLM can generate a podcast-style audio conversation between two AI hosts discussing your source material. It sounds like a gimmick. It is kind of a gimmick. But I listened to a 12-minute "podcast" about my research notes while commuting, and I actually retained more than when I skimmed the documents.

Is it essential? No. Did it help me process information during dead time? Absolutely. I've now used it three times — once for research review, twice for summarizing meeting notes before a follow-up call.

"The Audio Overview feature sounds like a party trick, but I've found it genuinely useful for reviewing meeting notes before follow-ups. Hearing two AI hosts debate my own notes is both entertaining and weirdly effective for retention." — My actual experience after 3 weeks

3. Cross-Document Connections I Would Have Missed

Here's a concrete example. I had uploaded a VentureBeat article about AI pricing trends and a separate Gartner research brief about enterprise software spending. These were in my "AI Tools" notebook alongside six other documents. I asked NotebookLM: "What themes connect these sources?"

It pointed out that both sources discussed the shift from per-seat pricing to usage-based models — something I had read in both documents separately but hadn't connected. It cited the exact paragraphs from each. That's the kind of insight that usually requires re-reading everything side by side, which I probably wouldn't have done.

Where NotebookLM Fell Flat (Sometimes Spectacularly)

1. It's Terrible at Unstructured, Messy Notes

I dumped a raw meeting transcript into one notebook — the kind where three people talk over each other, someone's dog barks in the background, and the conversation jumps between five topics. NotebookLM struggled. Badly.

The AI could extract basic topics, but it missed nuance — like the fact that we had agreed to table one topic and argued about another. The distinction between "we decided X" and "we argued about X and didn't decide" got flattened. For structured documents like PDFs and blog posts, it's excellent. For messy real-world notes, you still need a human brain.

2. No Mobile App. None.

As of July 2026, NotebookLM is web-only. No mobile app. No offline access. For a tool that's supposed to help you process information anywhere, this is a significant limitation. I wanted to review my research notes during a flight — couldn't. I wanted to quickly add a note from my phone — had to use the mobile browser, which is clunky.

3. The 50-Source Limit Can Be Tight

For the first two weeks, 50 sources per notebook felt generous. In week three, when I uploaded the full documentation set for a framework (dozens of pages across 6 PDFs, plus 12 blog tutorials, plus 4 GitHub READMEs), I hit the limit faster than expected. Each PDF counts as one source regardless of length, which is fine — but if you're doing deep research across many sources, you'll need to be strategic about which documents make the cut.

4. Organization Is Minimal

NotebookLM gives you notebooks. Inside each notebook, you add sources and notes. That's it. There are no folders, no tags, no nested structure, no way to link between notebooks. If you're used to Notion or Obsidian's organizational flexibility, NotebookLM feels barebones. It's a research tool, not a knowledge management system — and the line between those two things gets blurry fast.

NotebookLM vs. The Alternatives

vs. ChatGPT/Claude (with file upload): ChatGPT can analyze uploaded documents too. The difference is that NotebookLM only works with your sources and always cites them. ChatGPT might reference your document or might pull from its training data — you can't always tell. For research where accuracy matters, NotebookLM's source-grounding is more reliable.

vs. Notion AI: Notion is a much better note-taking app. NotebookLM is a much better research analysis tool. I've ended up using both: collect and organize in Notion, then export key documents to NotebookLM for deep analysis.

vs. Perplexity: Perplexity searches the web and cites sources. NotebookLM works with sources you provide. Different use cases — Perplexity for discovery, NotebookLM for analysis.

Who Should Use NotebookLM (And Who Shouldn't)

✅ NotebookLM is great for:

  • Students processing course materials and research papers
  • Researchers synthesizing information across multiple papers
  • Content creators researching topics for articles/videos
  • Professionals preparing for presentations or reports from source documents
  • Anyone who collects more PDFs than they actually read

❌ Skip NotebookLM if:

  • You need a general note-taking app (use Notion or Obsidian)
  • You primarily work on mobile
  • You need to process messy, unstructured meeting transcripts
  • You're looking for a ChatGPT replacement — it's a different kind of tool

Bottom Line

After three weeks, NotebookLM has earned a permanent spot in my workflow — but only for a specific slice of it. When I need to digest a pile of source material and extract insights, it's my go-to. The citation system makes it trustworthy in a way general-purpose AI chatbots aren't.

But it's not replacing my note-taking app. It's not my everyday AI assistant. And it definitely needs a mobile app. It's a specialized tool that does one category of task really well — and if that category matches your work, you'll wonder how you lived without it.
