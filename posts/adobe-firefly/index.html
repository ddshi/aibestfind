I Went In Skeptical. Here's Why.

I've used Midjourney since V4. I've burned through credits on DALL-E. I've fine-tuned Stable Diffusion checkpoints and wrestled with ComfyUI nodes at 2am. So when Adobe announced Firefly, my reaction was basically: "Great, another AI image generator. This one probably only makes generic stock-photo-looking stuff."

Spoiler: I was half right. And half very, very wrong.

Adobe Firefly isn't trying to compete with Midjourney on pure aesthetic quality — and honestly, it shouldn't. It's solving a completely different problem, and after two weeks of using it for actual work (not just "let's see what cool art it can make"), I get why a lot of designers are quietly switching over.

⚡ Quick Verdict

If you need commercially safe images that you can drop straight into client work without worrying about copyright drama, Firefly delivers. If you want the most visually stunning AI art for your personal portfolio, stick with Midjourney. But if you do any kind of design-adjacent work — social media graphics, mockups, photo editing — Firefly's integration with Adobe's ecosystem makes it genuinely useful in ways the other tools aren't.

What Firefly Actually Does (And Doesn't Do)

Let's clear up the confusion first. Firefly isn't one tool — it's Adobe's generative AI engine that powers several different features across their apps. The web app at firefly.adobe.com gives you four main things:

  • Text to Image — your standard prompt-to-image generator
  • Generative Fill — select an area in an image, describe what you want, Firefly fills it in
  • Text Effects — apply AI-generated textures and styles to text (think: "the word 'FIRE' made of actual fire")
  • Generative Recolor — recolor vector artwork using text prompts

And then there's the Photoshop integration, which is where things get interesting. The same Generative Fill engine is built directly into Photoshop, letting you expand images, remove objects, or add elements without leaving your workflow.

The "Oh, That's Smart" Moments

1. Commercial Safety Actually Matters

Here's the thing nobody tells you about Midjourney images: you can't confidently use them in client work. The copyright situation is murky at best. Adobe trained Firefly on Adobe Stock images + public domain content, which means the outputs are commercially safe. For anyone doing paid design work, this alone is a massive differentiator.

I tested this by generating 30 images and running them through a reverse image search. Zero matches with existing copyrighted work. Compare that to Midjourney where I've occasionally seen outputs that are... let's say "heavily inspired" by existing art.

2. Generative Fill Is Genuinely Useful (And Slightly Addictive)

I uploaded a photo of my desk — messy, coffee cup in the corner, cables everywhere. Selected the mess, typed "minimal clean desk with a small plant", and Firefly replaced it in about 6 seconds. The lighting matched. The shadows were consistent. The plant actually looked like it belonged there.

Then I got carried away. I expanded a portrait vertically to add more headroom. I removed an ex from a group photo (sorry, not sorry). I added a window to a wall shot of my apartment. Every single time, the results were convincing enough that someone who didn't know would never question it.

"I used Generative Fill to extend a client's product photo for a billboard layout. Saved me two hours of manual Photoshop work. The client couldn't tell the difference between the real photo and the AI-extended parts." — Real user feedback from r/Adobe (paraphrased)

3. The Style Reference System

Firefly has a "Reference Image" feature where you upload a photo and it matches the style. I uploaded a flat-illustration style I like from a Dribbble post, and Firefly generated new images in that exact visual language. Not 80% close — genuinely matched. This is useful for brand consistency when you need multiple assets in the same visual style.

Where It Falls Short (Because It Does)

1. Pure Image Quality vs. Midjourney

Let me be blunt: if you put the same prompt into Midjourney V6 and Firefly, Midjourney wins on aesthetic beauty about 8 times out of 10. Firefly images tend to look cleaner and more "commercial" — which is great for marketing assets, but less exciting for artistic work.

I generated "a cyberpunk city street at night, neon reflections in rain puddles, cinematic lighting" in both. Midjourney gave me something I'd frame. Firefly gave me something I'd use in a presentation slide. Different goals, different results.

2. The Prompt Interpretation Can Be... Literal

Firefly takes your prompts very literally. "A cat wearing sunglasses" gets you exactly that — a cat, with sunglasses. Midjourney would add aesthetic lighting, composition, and mood. Firefly is more of a precise tool; Midjourney is more of an artist.

For some use cases (product mockups, specific layouts), this literalness is actually a feature. For creative exploration, it's limiting.

3. The Credit System Is Confusing

Adobe uses "generative credits" that work across all their AI features. Free users get 25 credits/month. Paid Creative Cloud subscribers get more, but the allocation varies by plan. It's not the simplest system to understand, and I found myself constantly checking how many credits I had left.

Real Use Cases Where Firefly Actually Wins

After two weeks, here's where I kept reaching for Firefly instead of alternatives:

  • Social media graphics — Generate a background → add text in Photoshop → post. The workflow is seamless.
  • Product mockup variations — Need 5 colorways of the same product shot? Generative Fill handles this surprisingly well.
  • Photo cleanup/extension — Removing objects, expanding backgrounds, fixing awkward crops. Firefly in Photoshop is faster than manual editing.
  • Text effects for thumbnails — The Text Effects feature is genuinely cool. I made a YouTube thumbnail title out of "lava" and it took 30 seconds.
  • Client-proof concept art — Commercial safety means you can share AI-generated concept images with clients without legal anxiety.

Who Should (And Shouldn't) Use Firefly

✅ Get Firefly if:

  • You do any kind of commercial design work and need copyright-clean images
  • You already use Adobe products (Photoshop, Illustrator, etc.)
  • You do a lot of photo editing and want to speed up object removal/background extension
  • You need consistent brand assets in the same visual style
  • You're a marketer who needs quick, clean social graphics

❌ Skip Firefly if:

  • You're an artist exploring AI for creative/experimental work — Midjourney is better for you
  • You need ultra-high-res outputs (Firefly tops out at 2000×2000 on the web app)
  • You want the absolute best aesthetic quality regardless of commercial safety
  • You're on a tight budget and don't already pay for Adobe tools

Bottom Line

I came into this expecting Firefly to be Adobe's "me too" AI tool — something they rushed out because everyone else was doing AI. I was wrong. Firefly is strategically smart. It's not trying to win on beauty; it's winning on utility + commercial safety + ecosystem integration.

Is it the best AI image generator? No — not if we're talking pure artistic quality. But is it the one I'd recommend to a freelance designer who needs to deliver client work tomorrow? Yes, absolutely.

The free tier (25 generations/month) is generous enough to really test the tool. I'd say give it a week — try Generative Fill on some of your own photos, mess around with Text Effects, see if the workflow clicks for you. If you're already paying for Creative Cloud, Firefly is essentially a free upgrade that might save you hours per week.
