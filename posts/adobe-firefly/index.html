I Went In Skeptical. Here's Why.

I've used Midjourney since V4. I've burned through credits on DALL-E. I've fine-tuned Stable Diffusion checkpoints and wrestled with ComfyUI nodes at 2am. Spoiler: I was half right. And half very, very wrong.

The One Thing Firefly Does That Nobody Else Can

Let's get straight to the point: Generative Fill in Photoshop is the killer feature. Not the web app version — the actual Photoshop integration where you select a region, type what you want, and the AI fills it in with context-aware results.

I tested this with 14 different images across various scenarios:

  • Removing objects (8 tests): People in backgrounds, power lines, trash cans, photo bombers. Firefly matched the surrounding texture, lighting, and perspective correctly in 7 out of 8 cases. The one failure was a complex reflection on water where the AI created an unnatural mirror effect.
  • Adding objects (6 tests): "Add a dog sitting on the couch," "add clouds to an empty sky," "add a coffee cup on the desk." Firefly got 5 out of 6 right. The miss: the dog's shadow angle didn't match the room lighting, which was subtle but noticeable.

For comparison, I ran the same tasks through Midjourney's inpainting and Photoshop's old Content-Aware Fill. Midjourney produced more aesthetically pleasing results but with inconsistent lighting. Content-Aware Fill just didn't understand what "add a dog" meant — it could only blend existing pixels. Firefly was the only tool that both understood the semantic request AND matched the visual context.

Text to Image: Good, Not Great

This is where the "half right" part of my initial skepticism kicks in. For standalone image generation from text prompts, Firefly is solid but not spectacular.

I generated 50 images across different styles — photorealistic, illustration, 3D render, concept art, and graphic design. Then I ran the same prompts through Midjourney V6.1 for comparison.

Firefly's image quality is commercially adequate. It won't win art contests, but it will produce images you can confidently use in presentations, marketing materials, and client deliverables. For many professionals, that's exactly the right tradeoff.

The Commercial Safety Thing Is Real — And It Matters

Here's the part that changed my mind about Firefly. Adobe trained Firefly only on licensed content — Adobe Stock images, openly licensed content, and public domain works where copyright has expired. That means when you generate an image with Firefly, Adobe provides an IP indemnity for enterprise customers.

This matters more than most people realize. Midjourney, DALL-E, and Stable Diffusion were trained on vast internet scrapes that almost certainly include copyrighted images. The legal landscape is still being sorted out, but the risk for commercial users is real. Several lawsuits are ongoing.

I work with clients who need images for marketing campaigns, product packaging, and social media. The last thing they want is a copyright lawsuit because an AI accidentally reproduced someone else's work. Firefly eliminates that risk — or at least comes closer than any competitor.

Who Should Actually Use Firefly

After two weeks, here's my honest breakdown:

  • Graphic designers and marketing professionals: Firefly is the obvious choice. The Photoshop integration alone saves hours per project, and commercial safety is non-negotiable for client work.
  • Photographers doing retouching: Generative Fill replaces complex cloning and healing tasks. You'll still need manual work for detailed edits, but Firefly handles 80% of routine cleanup.
  • AI art enthusiasts chasing the best-looking images: Stick with Midjourney. Firefly's artistic ceiling is lower, and it doesn't give you the creative variety you probably want.
  • Small business owners making their own graphics: Firefly in Adobe Express is surprisingly accessible. You can make social media posts, flyers, and basic marketing materials without touching Photoshop.

Bottom Line

Firefly isn't trying to make the prettiest AI art. It's trying to make the most usable AI art for people who need commercial output. And on that metric, it's the best option right now.

If you already pay for Creative Cloud, you're leaving value on the table by not using it. If you don't, the free tier gives you enough to evaluate whether the commercial safety argument matters for your work. It probably does.
